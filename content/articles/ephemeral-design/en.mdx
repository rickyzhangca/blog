For a long time, digital product teams have treated a particular workflow as “best practice”: maintaining two highly detailed, mirror-image versions of the product as parallel sources of truth—one in Figma and one in code. But as design systems become increasingly over-engineered (and expensive to maintain), and as AI radically expands software development capacity, this dual-truth model is frustrating designers and slowing teams down.

**Ephemeral Design** offers a way out. It frees designers from the burden of maintaining two truths. Design files return to what they fundamentally are: drafts and communication artifacts. The design system remains the only durable asset, while designers and engineers collaborate directly in a code-centered environment.

---

## Chapter 1: The Problem of Two Truths

### 1.1 Design Was Never Meant to Be the Truth

The principle of a single source of truth says that any piece of information should be owned and edited in only one place so the system stays consistent. But for years, digital product development has violated this principle by effectively creating a dual-truth architecture:

* **Truth of Intent**: Lives in design tools like Figma and represents what the product *is supposed to* look like.
* **Truth of Implementation**: Lives in code repositories like GitHub and represents what the product *actually* looks like and *how it works*.

This split made sense when engineering was expensive. Changing code used to be slow and costly, so teams simulated and validated everything in a cheaper design environment first. But over time, that structure became a burden. When designers model every state, every response, and every logic branch at pixel-level fidelity, they are essentially building a parallel universe.

And design files are, by nature, a flawed source of truth. No matter how polished they look, they are still a **simulator**. They do not consider network latency, data complexity, browser rendering behavior, or accessibility tools. Code, on the other hand, is what users actually interact with. It is the only artifact that contains the full reality of the system.

### 1.2 The Cost of Alignment and Communication

A major pain point for many teams today is the cost of forcing design files to mirror the real product. That cost shows up as **communication overhead** and **alignment overhead**, and it does not grow linearly, but exponentially as products become more complex.

In traditional product development, designers often go back after a feature is already built (or nearly complete) and update the Figma file to match the final implementation. That is performative labor in the name of documentation consistency. It creates no user value. Its only purpose is to preserve the illusion that the design file is “the truth.”

#### Translation Loss

High-fidelity mockups often hide implementation complexity. A text input that looks simple in Figma may require backend validation, error handling, and compatibility work for legacy systems in code.

When designers hand off a fully polished static mock, it can create the impression that all decisions have already been made. Engineers may hold back technical objections early, only to raise them later when implementation constraints surface. That creates confusion and disruption midstream.

Designers build a perfect utopia in a vacuum. Engineers wrestle with reality and try to recreate that vision under constraints. Teams burn a huge amount of communication bandwidth on details like, “This shadow cannot be rendered that way on Android,” or “This data is not available when the list first loads.”

#### Drift Is Inevitable

Even top-tier teams with mature design systems cannot fully prevent design-code drift. One internal audit at Shopify found that even with a robust design system, 14% of implementations in a highly standardized admin interface had drifted from the system within a year.

That drift does not happen because engineers are careless. It happens because code is dynamic and environment-dependent, while design files are static and controlled. As long as design and code live separately, drift is inevitable; synchronization is only temporary.

### 1.3 Where the Industry Is Now

In many teams, the current collaboration model has started to resemble a quiet cold war between design and engineering.

Designers try to exert more control over implementation by introducing increasingly complex tooling in Figma, such as dev mode, variables, tokens, etc. Engineers, under pressure to ship quickly, often ignore that complexity and build something that looks close enough in code.

This does not just increase internal friction. More importantly, it blurs the definition of what the product actually is. When a bug appears, teams end up debating whether “the design file is wrong” or “the code is wrong,” instead of focusing on the only thing that matters: the user experience is broken.

## Chapter 2: How Figma Became a Trap

### 2.1 The Over-Engineering of Design Systems

Figma’s newer capabilities like okens, variables, modes, have made component systems more complex and increased overhead across the design industry. This has become a recurring theme in industry conversations since 2024.

Figma introduced capabilities for advanced logic with a good intention: to close the gap between design and code, and to make design files feel more like software systems. In practice, though, this often leads to **over-engineering**.

#### Cognitive Load Has Shifted to Designers

Designers are now expected to think more like software architects. Building and maintaining a deeply layered system requires strong abstraction skills. Designers have to mentally model dependency trees and manage variable mappings across multiple scenarios.

The result is that attention shifts away from user experience and innovation and toward system maintenance. Designers do not design as much anymore, they become Figma admins, spending time debugging broken variable bindings and conflicting component properties, instead of improving user flows or shaping product vision.

#### Fragile Logic, Weak Tooling

Worse, the logic structures inside Figma are fragile.

In a codebase, refactors are supported by IDEs, linting, and tests. In Figma, renaming a low-level variable can silently break hundreds of component connections across teams with no meaningful automated warnings. People do not discover the damage until they open the file and realize everything is broken. Repairing or rolling back those changes is far messier than it is in a version-controlled code environment.

This fragility makes designers overly cautious. Some avoid meaningful exploration because they are afraid of breaking the system. The tool that was supposed to amplify their capabilities becomes a constraint.

### 2.2 The ROI Breakdown

If all of this complexity in Figma translated directly into code or dramatically improved team-wide productivity, it might be worth the investment. But in most teams, it does not.

Very few organizations have a real automation pipeline that syncs Figma variables directly into the codebase, let alone anything more advanced.

So in practice, designers spend hours configuring interactions, hover states, responsive breakpoints, and other details in Figma, only for developers to ignore most of it during implementation. Engineers often look at the final visual and rebuild the UI from scratch in code to something they consider good enough.

That creates massive waste on both sides. A designer can spend 10 hours building an “intelligent” component in Figma, and the internal logic dies the moment it gets handed off. It becomes zombie logic: painstakingly built, but with no lasting value.

The work required to maintain perfect replicas inside Figma just creates frustration and drains motivation over time. This is one reason the design industry is walking into a crisis.

### 2.3 Economic and Psychological Pressure

Beyond workflow friction, economics are pushing this shift too. Pricing changes in tools like Figma have triggered widespread frustration.

For many teams, the cost of full-feature seats is getting harder to justify in terms of ROI. That forces a hard question: do we really need to maintain a large, complex “truth” in Figma where only designers live?

If the code repository (which the team already pays for and depends on) is the final source of truth, why are we investing so much budget and time in maintaining another one?

Psychologically, many designers are also hitting tool fatigue. Constant feature changes, plus the expectation that they now need to understand and design pseudo-programmatic logic, is leading to burnout. A lot of designers have not had the chance to truly immerse themselves in creative exploration for a long time.

The industry needs to get back to the two fundamentals of design: problem-solving and crafting.

## Chapter 3: Rebuilding Collaboration in the AI Era

### 3.1 The Cost of Code Is Falling Fast

AI is dramatically increasing engineering output, and it is changing the cost structure of product development.

In traditional software economics, **code was expensive and pixels were cheap**. So teams tried to validate ideas thoroughly in cheap pixels (design mocks) before writing expensive code.

But modern software engineering already lowered the cost of code through componentization, and now AI is pushing that even further. AI is making code **cheaper and faster** by enabling teams to:

* Generate working prototype code in minutes from natural-language prompts or rough sketches.
* Instantly assemble common screens using an existing component library or design system.

When a prototype that used to take three days can now be built in three hours, or even faster than creating a high-fidelity Figma mock (with layers, auto layout, and variable setup), the economic case for high-fidelity simulation in Figma starts to collapse.

### 3.2 Vibe Coding

In this model, creators do not need to write every line of code manually. They describe functionality and interaction behavior in natural language, and AI handles the implementation.

This approach still has limits. It will not replace full product development or solve every complex problem. But for designers, it is a major shift: they can now make simple changes directly, inspect the real product state, and experiment in a live environment, without needing to become traditional programmers or spend excessive time coordinating with engineering.

In reality, many top designers on high-performing teams are already working this way.

### 3.3 No Handoff

As AI accelerates software cycles and shortens development loops, the traditional design handoff process is starting to disappear. In its place, we are seeing tighter, more collaborative workflows between design and engineering, especially in fast-moving startups and small, agile teams.

In this model, design and development are no longer sequential upstream/downstream phases. They become parallel co-creation:

* **Early engineering involvement**: Engineers join during the design phase and use AI to rapidly build functional prototypes.
* **Continuous iteration**: Designers no longer wait until implementation is “done” to do QA. They make refinements directly in the development environment, often taking over fine-tuning work that used to sit with engineers.

This model eliminates alignment overhead and ensures product decisions are validated in a real code environment early, avoiding the “the design cannot be implemented” or “engineering shipped something before design caught up” situations.

## Chapter 4: A New Paradigm, Ephemeral Design

Based on all of the above, we are seeing the emergence of a new design-engineering paradigm.

Designers maintain very few design files and a lean but accurate design system. Actual feature design happens through **disposable draft files** that support fast iteration. This paradigm can be summarized as **Ephemeral Design**.

“Ephemeral” means design files are no longer permanent assets. They are process tools, not long-term records of truth.

The core principles of Ephemeral Design are:

* **Figma as a whiteboard**: In Figma, the priority is divergent thinking and clarifying logic. Teams use rough design drafts to explore multiple directions quickly. (“Rough” does not necessarily mean low-fidelity wireframes, it means bypassing the tedious work of setting up. Visually, these drafts may still look polished.)
* **Archive after launch**: Once a feature moves into development, the related design file loses maintenance value. It should be archived as historical context, not treated as the baseline for future changes. If the feature needs to be updated later, the team should reference the live product (the code), not a months-old mockup.
* **A lightweight design system is the only durable asset**: The only thing in Figma worth maintaining long-term is a lean component system that stays tightly aligned with code, so designers can assemble ideas quickly.

## Chapter 5: How to Put This Into Practice

### 5.1 Define the Boundaries Clearly

Before rolling out a No Handoff workflow, teams need to be honest about where Ephemeral Design works best.

Right now, it is most effective for teams that are strong in product logic, move quickly, and have a high bar for talent. In contexts where visual expression *is* the product (for example, marketing sites, landing pages, and brand-heavy experiences), fully abandoning traditional design deliverables requires more experimentation.

### 5.2 Concrete Steps for Teams

Teams leading this transition are typically adopting the following changes:

**1) Reshape the culture: redefine what a “draft” is**

* Make it explicit to stakeholders that Figma files are drafts and concept-validation tools, not final contracts.
* Create an archive workflow: at the end of each sprint, mark related design files as archived and stop maintaining them.
* Give more influence to designers with good taste, and those who have technical skills, and move the team toward outcome-driven decision-making.

**2) Put the design system on a diet**

* If a component or behavior does not exist in code, it should not exist in Figma.
* Tokens and variants in Figma should always stay synced to what exists in code.

**3) Encourage designers to embrace AI and the code environment**

* Encourage designers to use tools like Cursor or other AI-assisted coding tools.
* Do not frame touching code as crossing a line or taking on extra burden. Treat it as just another tool, use it when it helps.
* Improve developer experience so anyone involved can easily pull code, run Storybook, submit simple PRs, and spin up a staging environment for testing.

**4) Make code previews the new shared language**

* Encourage teams to use preview links from real code, either instead of, or alongside, Figma prototypes.
* Help everyone get comfortable reviewing and making decisions based on the real product experience.

### The Inevitable Return to First Principles

The shift toward Ephemeral Design is more than a workflow change. It is a healthy correction based on a deeper understanding of how modern product work actually happens.

The essence of design is **planning and problem-solving**, not **producing beautiful specification documents**.

At one time, high-fidelity mocks were a necessary way to reduce engineering risk. But as software engineering got faster and cheaper, and as the design complexity in Figma went up, the marginal value of the old process has fallen close to zero, and in many cases, gone negative.

Letting go of design as a source of truth is a form of **liberation** for designers. It frees them from a large amount of pseudo-design work and allows them to focus on higher-value areas: systems thinking, user insight, and product innovation.
